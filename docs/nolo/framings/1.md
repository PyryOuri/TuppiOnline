# NOLO is an imperfect-information team game over contingent continuations

Independent Gate 1 framing, 2026-09-05. No other framing was read. No existing
file was changed. This is an identification result, not a proposed playing policy
or a measurement of playing-strength improvement.

## Recommendation

**The governing area is dynamic game theory, specifically extensive-form team
games with imperfect information. The object to value is an information-legal
contingent continuation, including who gets the next lead.** Exact suit
combinatorics supplies constraints and reference probabilities inside that
object; it cannot supply the action value by itself.

This is a ranked recommendation, not an assertion that calling a card game a
game solves it. The substantive choice is to preserve three things together:

1. The remaining cards across **all four suits**, because discards move a card
   out of its suit without exposing it to a trick of that suit.
2. The player who will choose the next lead, because that choice determines
   which retained cards can become liabilities.
3. The information available when each future card is chosen, because a line
   must be executable without seeing the hidden layout.

Two reachable decisions make that choice concrete. At one, the shipped bot
throws `QD` from `3H QD 8S`; it should throw `8S`. Across **every one of the 20
legal hidden layouts and every legal continuation**, throwing `8S` leaves its
team on seven tricks. Keeping it permits nine, and forces nine in the actual
layout. At another, it ducks with `3C`; the better security line is **take with
`9C`, then lead `3C`**. Changing only the first card makes the current bot worse.

Those are different claims: the first is a policy-independent weak dominance
certificate with a strict improvement in the dealt layout; the second is a
better worst-case contingent line, not dominance against every opponent policy.

## The five tests for the recommendation

**1. Object and Gate 0 mapping.** Use a finite extensive-form game for the
resolved NOLO deal. Chance chooses a complete deal; a node retains the four
remaining hands, public showing/play history, current actor and partial trick,
team trick totals, and match score. Legal edges are exactly the engine's actions.
Completing a trick awards it to the highest card of the led suit and makes that
seat the next leader. There is no trump, dummy, or external discard pile of
undealt cards. Four compulsory NOLO shows reveal retained cards and sometimes
whole-hand exclusions. They are part of the information partition, not bids
that select a trump suit.

For seat `s`, identify nodes precisely when its own cards and public record are
the same. Its strategy must choose the same action distribution at those
indistinguishable nodes:

```
π_s : information sets of seat s → distributions over its legal actions
```

Seats 0 and 2 have the same utility; seats 1 and 3 have the opposing utility.
**Shared utility does not mean shared information.** Joint partnership planning
must still produce separate, executable seat strategies. For improving one seat
against a specified partner, hold that partner's strategy fixed instead. No
recommendation here assumes a partner can see the acting bot's hand or receive
an unobserved new plan midway through a deal.

At a leaf, apply `score_nolo` and `transition_rising_score`. The full match's
utility can be eventual match victory. A deal-only calculation then needs a
specified continuation value of the resulting match state, including the next
dealer where relevant. That continuation value is an additional model; it is
not supplied by the card rules. I use final team tricks and exact post-deal
score states below, and do not label either an estimated match-win probability.

This is consistent with the definition of information sets and shared-utility
teams in [Celli and Gatti, *Computational Results for Extensive-Form Adversarial
Team Games* (2018)](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/11462/11462-13-14990-1-2-20201228.pdf).
Their principal setting has one opposing player; NOLO has an opposing
partnership. Their communication-dependent solution methods therefore do not
transfer automatically. The mapping here is rules-derived, not a claimed
application of their solver.

**2. Assumptions.** The rules-derived representation needs only finite legal
play, the stated observations and scoring, and perfect recall within a deal.
These hold. It needs neither predictable low leads nor a model of ducking.
Selecting a numerical optimum additionally needs a criterion under uncertainty:
an opponent model, an equilibrium/security criterion, or explicitly bounded
uncertainty. The rules do not choose between exploitation and worst-case
protection. I recommend starting with certificates valid for all hard-feasible
layouts, then stating the criterion wherever those certificates stop.

**3. Prediction.** The current bot mistakes a card's ability to win its own
suit for the cost of retaining it along the ensuing line. It can therefore
discard the wrong winner, or refuse a useful voluntary win. In witness A,
`QD → 8S` is the concrete correction. In witness B, the correction is the
two-decision line `9C → 3C`, not an isolated change to following.

**4. Cheapest falsifier.** One engine-legal hidden assignment or continuation
in witness A that gives Team 0 another trick after `8S` falsifies its universal
certificate. One layout in witness B where the stated `9C → 3C` line needs
unavailable information or allows a fifth team trick falsifies that certificate.
I exhausted both tests below. The broader research priority would lose support
if further apparent continuation improvements mostly disappear when information
sets and the actual score utility are enforced. A faithful game representation
is not itself a falsifiable claim of useful computational compression.

**5. Policy robustness.** Retain all layouts consistent with card accounting,
follow-suit, remaining capacities, shown owners and mandatory showing exclusions.
Call this set `Ω_s`. It is a support set, not a posterior. A hard certificate
quantifies over `Ω_s` and legal opponent continuations, including unconventional
ones. In witness A it also quantifies over arbitrary partner and future own
actions. In witness B only seat 0's two specified choices are needed; partner
behaviour can be arbitrary. Neither uses a lead-rank likelihood. This survives
charter §6 more strongly than an assumed rational ducking rule does.

## Witness A: discard the card that would bring the lead back

Corpus deal index **31**, zero-based trick index **10**, second player to the
trick, **seat 2**. Team 0 already has seven tricks, Team 1 three. Starting match
score is `0–0`. Seat 1 has led `6C`; seat 2 is void in clubs and holds:

```
3H  QD  8S
```

The bot chooses `QD`. Its actual score vectors, in component order
`(shed_certain_winner, discard_liability, short_suit_bonus, rank_preference)`, are:

| Card | Score vector |
| --- | --- |
| `3H` | `(1, 0, 1, 3)` |
| `QD` | `(1, 2, 1, 12)` |
| `8S` | `(1, 0, 1, 8)` |

All three are highest unplayed in their suits. The inherited discard-liability
formula breaks the tie in favour of the queen. See
[`counting_components`](../../../src/tuppi_agents/counting.py) and
[`reactive_components`](../../../src/tuppi_agents/reactive.py). The shipped
[`VoidSeekingPartnerAwarePolicy`](../../../src/tuppi_agents/void_lead.py)
inherits this following/discard behaviour.

The public record says something stronger than those scalar liabilities:

- Seat 1 showed `4S`, which it has not played.
- Seat 1 proved void in hearts and diamonds. Its two cards after leading `6C`
  are therefore either `{2S,4S}` or `{4C,4S}`.
- Seat 3 proved void in spades. Seat 0 proved void in clubs.
- Apart from seat 2's `8S`, the only remaining spades are `2S,4S`.

**Discard `8S`.** Seat 1 must win the current club trick: only the lower `4C`
can remain elsewhere. It can then lead only clubs or spades. It wins both
remaining tricks too: nobody retains a spade above its `4S`, and its possible
`4C` is the last club. Seat 2's `3H` and `QD` leave as harmless discards.

**Discard `QD`.** Keeping `8S` leaves a route for a spade lead to put seat 2
back on lead. After that, its remaining `3H` takes another trick. In the actual
remaining layout, every legal continuation does exactly this:

| Seat | Remaining cards at the decision, after seat 1's lead |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | `5D 6D JD` |
| 1 | `2S 4S` |
| 2 | `3H QD 8S` |
| 3 | `2H 10D 4C` |

This is not an inference that a low card was led from a short suit. The relevant
ownership, voids, and residual suit restrictions are hard public facts.

### Exhaustive check and its exact scope

There are 560 partitions of the eight unseen remaining cards into the three
hands of sizes 3, 2, 3 before imposing public evidence. I replayed **all 560**
through `hypothetical_continuation`; the engine accepted 20 and rejected 540.
The accepted set exactly matched the independently enumerated
`HardConstraintModel` set and its exact assignment count.

For every accepted assignment I traversed **all legal remaining actions through
the ordinary engine**, without equivalent-card pruning or an opponent model:

| Root discard | Terminal team-0 totals over all legal continuations | Number of terminal paths |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| `8S` | **7 only** | 1,680 |
| `QD` | 7, 8, or 9 | 1,200 |
| `3H` | 7, 8, or 9 | 1,200 |

Path counts are enumeration counts, **not probabilities**. In the actual layout,
`QD` has 24 terminal paths, all ending at nine; `8S` has 48, all ending at seven.
The perfect-information solver also returns seven versus nine in each of the
20 layouts, but the all-actions enumeration is the stronger evidence.

Team 0 cannot finish below the seven it has already taken. Thus `8S` achieves
the absolute lower bound in every feasible world regardless of subsequent
behaviour. No posterior, strategic signal, favourable partner, or future search
is needed to justify this particular decision. It weakly dominates keeping the
spade, with strict improvement in the dealt world and other compatible worlds.

The endpoint matters even though the deal is already lost. At `0–0`, seven
tricks concedes **4** points; nine concedes **12**. Applied to the same legal
position with Team 0 down at `0–40`, the outcomes are `0–44` and `0–52`: the
discard can prevent an immediate match loss. That is a rules-level score
transition comparison, not a newly simulated match. If Team 0 were up, both
losses would reset the score and the margin improvement would have no value.

## Witness B: an avoidable win can be the correct first move

Corpus index **6**, trick index **10**, fourth player, **seat 0**. Team totals
are `(3,7)`, starting match score `0–0`. The current trick is:

```
seat 1: 6C    seat 2: 6H    seat 3: 5C    seat 0: to play
seat 0 holds 9C 3H 3C
```

The bot's `safe_loss_preference` ranks `3C` above `9C`: their full vectors are
`(2,0,3)` and `(0,0,9)` respectively. It chooses `3C`.

There are exactly two compatible hidden layouts:

| Layout | Seat 1 | Seat 2 | Seat 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | `2C 7C` | `5H 3S` | `4C 10C` |
| B, actually dealt | `7C 10C` | `5H 3S` | `2C 4C` |

Shown and still retained `7C` fixes one owner; shown `4C` fixes another. The
partner's current heart discard proves its club void. Capacity and the remaining
cards force the partner to hold `5H 3S`; the uncertainty is only where `2C` and
`10C` sit. I checked all 90 unconstrained partitions: engine replay accepted
exactly these two. No behavioural interpretation of the shows was used.

The existing suit mathematics applies cleanly **as a component** here:

```
general_split_distribution(
    outstanding=4, hand_sizes=(2,2,2), void=(False,True,False)
) == {(2,0,2): Fraction(1,1)}
```

I also enumerated `suits.layouts([3,9,13])`, seat 0's initial club holding, and
filtered on the played clubs, remaining lengths, and shown owners. It leaves
the two layouts with exact reference weights `1/2,1/2`. These are the opening
deal law conditioned on the specified hard constraints, **not** a posterior
conditioned on all chosen actions. The decision below does not need the weights.

**Take `9C`, then lead `3C`.** Team 0 takes the current trick, reaching four.
Both opponents retain only clubs; one necessarily beats `3C`. An opponent then
leads clubs again on the final trick, while both friendly hands discard off
suit. Team 0 finishes at **four**, whichever opponent has `10C` and whatever
cards either opponent or the partner chooses. The same own continuation works
in both layouts; there is no hidden-state-dependent decision.

**Duck `3C`.** Opponents can retain `10C` and play their lower clubs on the next
trick. Seat 0 must play `9C`, win, and then lead its last card, `3H`. Either it
or its partner takes that final trick. Team 0 finishes at **five**. This is an
information-legal opposing line in both layouts, not an omniscient choice of
different strategies. If the opponents instead spend `10C` early, they can
allow Team 0 to finish at three. Therefore the take is a better security line,
not a universally better exploit.

All legal paths, per layout, were:

| Root and own next lead | Final Team 0 tricks | Paths per layout |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| `9C`, then `3C` | 4 | 8 |
| `9C`, then `3H` | 6 | 4 |
| `3C`, all subsequent legal play | 3 or 5 | 4 each |

The current bot follows the second row after a forced root `9C`: its shortest
suit/lowest rank tie is broken toward hearts. On the actual layout, replaying
its unmodified continuation gives:

| Line | Final Team 0 tricks | Points won |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Bot's actual `3C` and continuation | 5 | 8 |
| Change only root to `9C`, retain bot continuation | 6 | 4 |
| Information-legal `9C → 3C` | 4 | 12 |

These are three branch diagnostics on one position, not an A/B policy experiment.
They explain why a superficially promising one-card correction can measure
negative: the useful object contains a continuation commitment. At a hypothetical
starting score `40–0`, four tricks reaches `52–0`; five reaches `48–0`. At
`0–40`, all these winning margins reset to `0–0` and this witness provides no
score benefit.

### A necessary correction to charter §6's rationality claim

The charter's rule that an avoidable win reveals no lower card is valid only
under an additional **local duck-if-possible** behavioural assumption. It is
not entailed by rational pursuit of the partnership's final objective.

Witness B is a counterexample under a worst-case objective: the actor holds a
lower club and should deliberately win. In fact `9C` is publicly shown. An
opponent treating its play as proof of no lower club would infer a false fact
about a rational continuation. This leaves the charter's central warning about
policy uncertainty intact, but narrows its proposed robust inference tier.
The charter is left unchanged, as required.

For an opponent model that sometimes concedes both late tricks after a duck,
write `q` for that outcome's probability. In this particular position the duck's
expected team total is `5−2q`, versus exactly four for the take-and-club line.
The ranking changes at `q=1/2` when valuing expected tricks or the linear winning
points at `0–0`. No value of `q` was fitted. This identifies precisely the
behavioural assumption needed to prefer exploitation to security.

## Why inference alone, or a perfect-information oracle, is insufficient

A useful negative diagnostic came from corpus index **1**, trick index **10**,
fourth player, seat 2. Its hand is `KC 2D 10D`, facing
`seat 3:7S, seat 0:6S, seat 1:JD`. The bot discards `KC`. In the actual layout,
the exact solver gives six own-team tricks for `KC` and five for `10D`.
It would be easy to report another bot mistake.

But the actor has 24 hard-feasible layouts. Exhaustive enumeration gave:

| Exact own-team minimax total after `KC` | After `10D` | Layouts |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4 | 6 | 6 |
| 4 | 5 | 6 |
| 6 | 5 | 6 |
| 6 | 6 | 3 |
| 4 | 4 | 3 |

Under the uniform-feasible **reference**, their averaged perfect-information
values are `19/4 = 4.75` for `KC`, `21/4 = 5.25` for `10D`: the ranking reverses.
That reference is not asserted to be the correct posterior, nor are those
averages the imperfect-information game value. This position is rejected as a
policy-independent certificate against `KC`.

There are two separate difficulties. First, the posterior needs the likelihood
of the public actions under the players' policies, including showing/contract
selection. Second, even the right posterior does not permit different future
own actions in indistinguishable worlds. Exact probabilities do not fix that
decision constraint. The distinction is also explicit in
[`tuppi_exact/sampling.py`](../../../src/tuppi_exact/sampling.py): its uniform
feasible sampler expressly does not claim to be a posterior.

This is why the relevant quantifier is **one executable continuation working
across compatible worlds**, rather than a separately optimal continuation in
each world. Averaging perfect-information solutions can violate that order.
[Long et al., *Understanding the Success of Perfect Information Monte Carlo
Sampling in Game Tree Search* (2010)](https://www.cs.du.edu/~sturtevant/papers/pimc.pdf)
describe this as strategy fusion, while also showing that PIMC can work well in
some games. I did not measure strategy-fusion error in NOLO here. The 24-layout
diagnostic establishes hidden-layout sensitivity, a different issue; the cited
paper explains the additional danger in treating an average oracle as exact.

## Alternatives considered and rejected as the governing object

These are the alternatives actually considered, in decreasing practical
relevance. Rejection is of the stated mapping, not of every possible future
generalisation of an entire field. Each gets the five required tests.

### 1. Exact combinatorial probability and suit order statistics

**Object.** A probability law on outstanding suit allocations and contributions
on successive leads; map it to `suits.layouts`, split laws, and the named
`LOWEST`/`HIGHEST` play-out models. This is the best supporting mathematics here.

**Assumptions.** The exact answers are conditional on the specified dealing
constraints and, for trick laws, the named contribution rule and lead count.
Using them as full action values additionally needs the suit's future exposure
and discards to be represented. That additional condition does not hold for an
isolated suit model. I accept the charter's established closed forms and prior
negative results; I did not retest them.

**Prediction.** Refine the cost of retaining/discarding `QD` versus `8S` by their
future exposure. With a fixed exposure schedule, both look like winners when
reached. The critical fact in witness A is that discarding `8S` prevents hearts
and diamonds from being led at all. A stand-alone holding law does not choose
that schedule, so it cannot justify the correction without adding the game.

**Falsifier.** Any root action that changes which suits are led invalidates an
action comparison that holds those exposures fixed. Witnesses A and B supply
such examples. Conversely, a proved sufficient suit-only statistic reproducing
these action values would weaken my claim that joint continuations must remain
explicit.

**Policy robustness.** Hard-feasible layout restrictions survive. The named
contribution policies and rank-choice likelihoods do not survive unknown play
unchanged. A precise reference law is valuable but not automatically a posterior.
**Verdict:** retain as a component; reject as the governing decision object.

### 2. Bayesian decision theory with a fixed-policy environment

**Object.** A belief over full hidden hands, updated from observed actions, with
future choices optimized under specified partner/opponent policies. For one
controlled seat this can be formulated as a partially observed decision process;
controlling a partnership also requires separate private observation histories.

**Assumptions.** A deal prior and an action likelihood are needed. Given those,
Bayesian updating is appropriate. The unknown policies in charter §6 mean the
likelihood is not supplied by legality. The artificial forced-NOLO corpus also
does not license a natural-NOLO posterior.

**Prediction.** Infer whether a putative winner can escape, and change a discard
such as `KC` to `10D` when the conditional continuation values warrant it. The
24-layout reversal shows that the choice can depend on the hidden-layout weights;
witness B identifies another sensitivity through `q=1/2`.

**Falsifier.** A supposed hard exclusion contradicted by a legal action under
another opponent policy falsifies the policy-free reading of the update. The
avoidable `9C` win with a retained `3C` is an explicit example. For a declared
likelihood model, a changed action ranking across plausible models is the cheap
robustness test; not a proof that Bayesian mathematics itself is wrong.

**Policy robustness.** It survives only by stating the likelihood assumptions,
varying them, or retaining a set of candidate models. The support-only
certificates above avoid that dependency. **Verdict:** a potentially useful
conditional exploit model; reject a single guessed posterior as the governing
policy-robust object. No Bayesian scheme or fitted likelihood was implemented.

### 3. Perfect-information minimax as a substitute for the information game

**Object.** Solve the four known hands and rank root actions by adversarial
continuations. This is a restriction of the recommended area, implemented by
`solve_late_game`; averaging it over deals is an approximation, not a new area.

**Assumptions.** All future players can distinguish the hands used by the search.
That holds for a research oracle and generally fails at a playing seat. A
partnership must not silently become a controller that sees both hands.

**Prediction.** It correctly points to `8S` and to `9C → 3C`, but also points to
`10D` in the actual index-1 layout. Only the first two have been promoted here
beyond a full-information diagnostic, using separate certificates.

**Falsifier.** Opposite preferred continuations at indistinguishable nodes
invalidate an oracle line as executable. A root ranking reversal across feasible
layouts, as at index 1, already falsifies a layout-independent recommendation.
It does not by itself establish strategy fusion.

**Policy robustness.** Adversarial choices remove the need to know an opponent's
policy **after granting information**; they do not justify granting it. Nor do
they identify a posterior over initial worlds. **Verdict:** retain the exact
solver as a diagnostic and certificate aid, reject it as the whole game object.
This is not a re-run or a claimed explanation of the historical PIMC result.

### 4. Static matching or network flow for disposing of liabilities

**Object.** Match dangerous cards to future off-suit discard opportunities;
capacity is one card per seat per trick, and a void creates an eligible disposal
slot. This is an appealing relaxation of the observed suit flows.

**Assumptions.** A static formulation needs the available future slots and their
costs to be given independently of the matching. Here the retained cards can win
the lead and change the suit of all subsequent slots. Partner and opponent
actions also constrain which proposed slots can coexist.

**Prediction.** Reserve safe exits for expensive cards. In witness A, a
rank-priced assignment wants to dispose of `QD`; it misses that disposing of
`8S` creates guaranteed exits for both other cards. The valuable change is in the
existence of the slots, not a better pairing within a fixed set.

**Falsifier.** Two choices with different reachable discard-slot schedules
falsify the fixed-network mapping; witness A supplies them. Time-expanding the
network with branches for actors, hidden hands and observations can repair the
mapping, but that reintroduces the recommended game object.

**Policy robustness.** A proven available slot is policy-independent. A predicted
future opponent lead is not. **Verdict:** possible bound or relaxation, not the
governing model. No standalone flow optimisation was computed.

### 5. Classical misère combinatorial game theory via independent suit sums

**Object.** Treat suits as separate component games and combine their values,
with an inverted terminal convention suggested by “avoid tricks.”

**Assumptions.** The proposed sum needs moves to stay within a chosen component
and component options to survive independently. Its usual terminal misère
interpretation would also have to match the reward. Both mappings fail: an
off-suit discard consumes a card in another component; the winner controls the
next component; all 52 cards are always played; and utility depends on the team
split and rising score, not on who makes the last move.

**Prediction.** This mapping would seek a different card from a combined suit
value or terminal parity. It cannot produce a justified correction to `QD` or
`3C` without adding the missing coupled transitions and scoring. It therefore
fails the task's actionable-prediction test; inventing a card recommendation
from it would overstate the mapping.

**Falsifier.** A single legal off-suit discard falsifies the independent-component
mapping. The different totals in witness B after the same number of remaining
plays also show why move-count parity is not the objective.

**Policy robustness.** The mapping fails even with every hand visible, so
policy-independent component algebra cannot rescue it. **Verdict:** reject this
specific classical sum/terminal mapping. This does not rule out a future
generalised scoring-game theory that explicitly models the missing structure.

## Rules audit: two Gate 0 deductions need qualification

I checked the mappings against both
[`project_target_v1.md`](../../rules/project_target_v1.md) and the executable
[`scoring.py`](../../../src/tuppi_rules/scoring.py),
[`match.py`](../../../src/tuppi_rules/match.py),
[`trick_play.py`](../../../src/tuppi_rules/trick_play.py), and
[`showing.py`](../../../src/tuppi_rules/showing.py). Two deductions in
`game_object.md` must not be used literally:

- **§5: the losing team's additional tricks do change the points.** With 13
  fixed tricks, `score_nolo(8,5)` awards Team 1 eight points;
  `score_nolo(9,4)` awards it twelve. You cannot increase one total while holding
  the other constant. Avoiding an own trick still reduces the opponent's margin
  while losing; whether that margin matters is a match-transition question.
- **§6: only the margin of a down-team *win* is erased.** A down-team loss's
  margin can determine whether the up team reaches 52. At `0–40`, the preceding
  splits produce `0–48` and terminal `0–52`. Likewise, up-team winning margins
  cease to matter after the threshold is reached. The source rules and Phase C
  already represent these distinctions correctly.

These qualifications are directly relevant to witness A, which improves the
margin of an already lost deal. All original documents remain untouched.

## What was checked, and what was not

Read first: the charter and game object, authoritative rules and executable
reference; then the valuation package's suit, play, corpus and designed-deal
interfaces, Phase A, Phase C, holding-valuation documentation, and the relevant
parent handoff sections. Traced the current lead/follow/discard rankings, the
observation boundary, hard constraints, exact counting, hypothetical replay,
and late-game solver. `designed` was inspected but not needed: the fixed random
corpus supplied non-vacuous positions. The established hypotheses in charter
§§4–5 were not re-estimated.

Before computation, a scratch preregistration fixed:

```
CorpusSpec(
  profile_key="partner_aware_v1", deal_count=32,
  root_seed="nolo-framing-2026-09-05", starting_score=(0,0),
  initial_dealer=0, condition=ContractCondition.FORCED_NOLO
)
```

`CorpusSpec` validates only registered Phase 4 profiles. Consequently I used its
fields with `generate_base_deal`, `DealerPolicy.ROUND_ROBIN`, engine matches,
and `forced_show_action`, but explicitly used **`void_seeking_agents()` at all
four seats** for play. Calling `iter_results(spec)` alone would instead play
`partner_aware_v1` and would not reproduce this corpus. No registry was changed.

The predeclared screen was every decision with at least two legal actions and
at most three tricks remaining. Priority was voluntary-win, partner-overtake,
then lead/discard witnesses. All 32 deals were completed under the shipped bot;
no alternate seed was searched.

`walk_deal` produced 1,664 per-card records and 512 suit-flow rows;
`verify_flow_conservation` passed on every deal. There were 382 follower

The other screened positions retained only the actual-layout solver diagnostic.
Index 26's lead ranking reversed across worlds. Index 3's discard ranking also

For the two principal witnesses I additionally enumerated **unconstrained**
remaining-card partitions and let engine replay determine legality, rather than
trusting the hard model not to exclude a possible world. Both accepted sets
matched exactly. This was a deliberate non-vacuity and support-completeness
check. The immediate-only change in witness B was a further negative control.

No policy, learning system, likelihood model, equilibrium algorithm, or flow
solver was implemented. No A/B trial, match simulation, human-data analysis,
performance benchmark, or estimate of general strength gain was run. No
confidence interval is attached to the screen: its observations share deals
and were used to find witnesses, not to estimate an independent success rate.
No full imperfect-information equilibrium was computed. The external literature
check was limited to the cited primary papers and nearby formal definitions;
it was not a systematic review of NOLO literature. No remembered published
result is presented as verified evidence.

## Next work if this framing is right

1. Pre-register a small family of information-set positions and certificate
   endpoints. Keep the hard-fact tier separate from optional action likelihoods;
   specifically, do not hard-exclude lower cards after an avoidable win.
2. Represent candidate **continuation outcomes**, including next-leader control
   and discard exits, over the compatible layouts. Merge future decisions only
   when the acting seat genuinely cannot distinguish the histories. Start with
   the small late-game cases where exact checks can expose modelling errors.
3. Use the existing suit laws and card/seat capacity counts to reduce the state
   space and provide bounds. Require any compression to preserve the two
   witnesses and the index-1 negative diagnostic. Do not assume additive holding
   values imply additive decision values.
4. When universal certificates stop deciding the action, report the remaining
   ambiguity. Compare a stated security criterion with explicit opponent-model
   sensitivity, and value the actual score transitions. Any later empirical
   strength test belongs to a separate, paired, pre-registered programme.

The immediate mathematical target is a tractable representation of these
information-legal outcome sets. It is not another rank weight. Whether that
representation can extend far enough into the deal to improve a practical bot
remains open.

## Confidence and what would change it

**High confidence in the game object and the two finite certificates.** They
come from reachable engine states, exhaustive legality checks, and explicit
continuations. Witness A already satisfies the actionable criterion without
knowing opponents' policies. Witness B shows why the useful correction can
require two linked decisions and why local rationality inference is unsafe.

**Moderate confidence that this is the most productive research direction;
low confidence in any implied size of eventual strength gain.** The useful
certificates were late and heavily constrained. The search was deliberately
small, and the general formalism is expensive. I have not found a theorem making
most NOLO decisions easy.

What would most change the recommendation is evidence that almost all valuable
earlier decisions require accurate policy likelihoods while hard-information
continuation reasoning rarely separates actions, or a much smaller mathematical
state that provably preserves the same decisions. That would change the research
priority toward inference or compression. It would not erase the concrete
counterexamples to isolated liability scoring.

The following standalone Python reproduces the two witnesses without regenerating
the corpus. It preserves initial hand order for canonical showing, replays the
public prefix, enumerates all unconstrained remaining partitions, verifies the
hard-model support count, and enumerates every terminal path. It implements no
playing policy. Prefix groups are tricks in chronological order; the final group
is the partial trick. Actors and leaders are obtained from the engine.

```python
from collections import Counter
from itertools import combinations

from tuppi_engine import (
    ExplicitDeal, EnginePhase, PlayCardAction, apply_action,
    create_deal_state, observation_for,
)
from tuppi_engine.errors import EngineError
from tuppi_rules import (
    FULL_DECK, Contract, MatchScore, card_from_code, card_to_code, card_to_index,
)
from tuppi_rules.errors import RulesError
from tuppi_agents.void_lead import void_seeking_agents
from tuppi_valuation.corpus import forced_show_action
from tuppi_exact.constraints import HardConstraintModel
from tuppi_exact.counting import feasible_assignment_count
from tuppi_exact.sampling import HiddenHandAssignment
from tuppi_exact.continuation import hypothetical_continuation
from tuppi_exact.errors import ContinuationError

cases = [
    (31, 3, [
        "AD 5D 9C KS 6H AS 6D 7H JD 7S 3D KD AH",
        "AC QS 4D 4S 10H KH 2S 6S 7C KC 9S 6C 10C",
        "3H 5H JS 8C 10S QD 8H 2C 9H 3S 8D 8S 9D",
        "10D 5C 7D 2H JC JH 4H 2D 4C 3C 5S QC QH",
    ], [
        "9C 7C 8C 5C", "6H 10H 9H QH", "5S AS QS JS",
        "7H KH 8H JH", "4D 8D 7D AD", "AH AC 5H 4H",
        "7S 6S 3S QC", "KS 9S 10S JC", "3D KC 9D 2D",
        "2C 3C KD 10C", "6C",
    ]),
    (6, 2, [
        "3D 9H AH 10H KS 2D 9C 8D KC 3H JD 3C 8S",
        "QC 8H 9D 7C KD 5D 10S 10C JS QS 7D 7S 6C",
        "6D 5S 9S AC 3S 4D JH 10D 6S 6H 5H 4S 7H",
        "QH KH QD 4C 2C JC 2S 8C 4H AD AS 2H 5C",
    ], [
        "2S 8S 7S 9S", "AC JC KC QC", "4D AD JD KD",
        "QD 8D 9D 10D", "AS KS QS 6S", "2H AH 8H JH",
        "2D 7D 6D KH", "5D 7H QH 3D", "10S 5S 4H 10H",
        "JS 4S 8C 9H", "6C 6H 5C",
    ]),
]

def all_partitions(obs, remaining_sizes):
    played = {p.card for t in obs.completed_tricks for p in t.plays}
    played |= {p.card for p in obs.current_trick}
    unseen = set(FULL_DECK) - played - set(obs.own_hand)
    seats = [s for s in range(4) if s != obs.observing_seat]
    for first in combinations(sorted(unseen, key=card_to_index),
                              remaining_sizes[seats[0]]):
        left = unseen - set(first)
        for second in combinations(sorted(left, key=card_to_index),
                                   remaining_sizes[seats[1]]):
            hands = [None] * 4
            hands[obs.observing_seat] = obs.own_hand
            hands[seats[0]], hands[seats[1]] = first, second
            hands[seats[2]] = tuple(sorted(left - set(second), key=card_to_index))
            owners = {c: s for s in seats for c in hands[s]}
            yield HiddenHandAssignment(obs.observing_seat, tuple(hands), owners, 0)

def terminal_counts(state, team):
    if state.phase is not EnginePhase.TRICK_PLAY:
        return Counter({state.team_tricks[team]: 1})
    counts = Counter()
    for action in observation_for(state, state.current_actor).legal_actions:
        counts.update(terminal_counts(apply_action(state, action), team))
    return counts

for index, dealer, hands, prefix in cases:
    ref = f"framing-witness:{index}"
    state = create_deal_state(
        ExplicitDeal(dealer=dealer, hands=tuple(
            tuple(card_from_code(c) for c in h.split()) for h in hands)),
        starting_score=MatchScore(0, 0), match_ref=ref,
        deal_ref=f"{ref}/deal:0", deal_number=0, event_start_index=0,
    )
    while state.phase is EnginePhase.SHOWING:
        obs = observation_for(state, state.current_actor)
        state = apply_action(state, forced_show_action(obs, Contract.NOLO))
    for group in prefix:
        for c in group.split():
            state = apply_action(state, PlayCardAction(
                seat=state.current_actor, card=card_from_code(c)))
    obs = observation_for(state, state.current_actor)
    chosen = void_seeking_agents()[state.current_actor].choose_action(obs)
    assert card_to_code(chosen.card) == ("QD" if index == 31 else "3C")
    results = {card_to_code(a.card): Counter() for a in obs.legal_actions}
    accepted, attempted = 0, 0
    club_line = Counter()
    for assignment in all_partitions(obs, tuple(map(len, state.hands))):
        attempted += 1
        try:
            world = hypothetical_continuation(obs, assignment).state
        except (RulesError, EngineError, ContinuationError):
            continue
        accepted += 1
        for action in obs.legal_actions:
            after = apply_action(world, action)
            results[card_to_code(action.card)].update(
                terminal_counts(after, obs.observing_seat % 2))
            if index == 6 and card_to_code(action.card) == "9C":
                after = apply_action(after, PlayCardAction(
                    seat=0, card=card_from_code("3C")))
                club_line.update(terminal_counts(after, 0))
    assert accepted == feasible_assignment_count(HardConstraintModel.build(obs))
    assert (attempted, accepted) == ((560, 20) if index == 31 else (90, 2))
    if index == 31:
        assert results["8S"] == Counter({7: 1680})
        assert results["QD"] == Counter({7: 360, 8: 360, 9: 480})
    else:
        assert results["9C"] == Counter({4: 16, 6: 8})
        assert results["3C"] == Counter({3: 8, 5: 8})
        assert club_line == Counter({4: 16})
    print(index, "partitions", attempted, "legal", accepted,
          "bot", card_to_code(chosen.card), "outcomes", results)
```
